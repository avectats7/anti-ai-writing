# Discourse Rewrites: How to Fix the Structural Layer

`discourse-tells.md` says what to look for. This file says what to do about it, with worked pairs in the formats people actually write: email, chat, LinkedIn, client update, landing page.

Layer 1 fixes are substitutions. Layer 2 fixes are moves: cut a paragraph, reorder two, add a name, leave something open. They change the shape of the piece, which means they carry more risk. Two guardrails before anything below.

**Guardrail 1: never fake the mess.** Inventing a tangent for a client email, or a fake uncertainty in a proposal, is worse than the tell it replaces. If there is no real aside, do not manufacture one. Cut instead of adding.

**Guardrail 2: the fix is subtraction first.** Six of the eight habits are fixed by deleting something. Try deletion before you try addition.

**Guardrail 3: stay inside the budget.** Under 40 words, zero additions. Under 200, one. Under 800, two. Above 800, three. The length bands and what applies in each are in `discourse-tells.md`.

## Fix 1: cut the stated takeaway

The move: delete the final interpretive sentence. Nothing replaces it.

Before:
> The March cohort converted at 4.1% against 2.3% in February. We changed the landing page headline and cut two form fields. Small changes to the entry point can have an outsized effect on the whole funnel.

After:
> The March cohort converted at 4.1% against 2.3% in February. We changed the landing page headline and cut two form fields.

The reader can do the arithmetic. The third sentence was the model reassuring itself that the point had landed.

Variant: if the takeaway carries information the numbers do not, keep the information and cut the moral.

Before:
> ...which shows that reducing friction pays off.

After:
> ...and we're doing the same to the demo request form next week.

## Fix 2: let one thread hang, under contract

The move: keep the one real thing that does not serve the main argument, instead of editing it out. Most drafts started with it and lost it during tidying.

An open item in a piece someone is paying for carries a contract. Three fields, all of them:

1. **Owner.** Who is on it. Naming yourself counts. Naming nobody does not.
2. **Date.** When the reader hears about it again.
3. **Consequence.** What it does and does not affect, so the reader knows whether to worry.

Missing any of the three, the item gets cut, not kept. A loose end without an owner is not candour, it is the writer handing the reader a problem. This is the difference between reporting an open issue and confessing one.

Before:
> Hi Marc, following up on the Q3 report. Impressions are up 22%, CPC is flat, and conversions are up 14%. I've attached the breakdown by campaign. Let me know if you'd like to discuss.

After:
> Hi Marc, following up on the Q3 report. Impressions are up 22%, CPC is flat, conversions up 14%. Breakdown by campaign attached.
>
> Separately, call tracking still isn't firing on the Spanish pages, so the lead count is a floor rather than a total. It doesn't affect the CPA figure. I'm on it and I'll have a cause or a workaround by the 29th.

The second paragraph does not serve the first. That is the point. It also names an owner, a date, and what the problem does and does not touch, which is what keeps it from reading as an apology.

## Fix 3: stop manufacturing closure

The move: find the sentence where the writer takes control of the outcome and check whether it is true. If it is not, replace it with the actual state.

Before:
> After looking at the data I've decided to pause the brand campaign and reallocate to search.

After (if the decision is not actually made):
> I want to pause the brand campaign and move the budget to search. I'm not certain: brand is the only thing feeding the remarketing pool, and I don't have a replacement for that yet.

Before:
> Once I understood that the problem was scope, everything else fell into place.

After:
> The scope was part of it. The rest I still can't explain.

## Fix 4: name the feeling

The move: replace the physical description with the plain word.

| Body report | Named |
|---|---|
| my stomach dropped when the numbers came in | the numbers scared me |
| there was a knot in my chest for the rest of the day | I was anxious about it all day |
| the room went very quiet | nobody wanted to answer |
| I felt the weight of it settle | it's been sitting badly with me |
| a strange lightness came over me | I was relieved |

Keep the physical version only when the physical thing happened and matters: "the room went quiet" is fine if you are describing what a room actually did in a meeting you are reporting on.

## Fix 5: open on content

The move: delete the first paragraph and read the second. If the piece still starts, the first paragraph was staging.

"On content" is not a synonym for "on a number." Seven openers work, and across any set of pieces you should use at least three of them:

| Opener | Example |
|---|---|
| The number | "CPA is $58, down from $71." |
| The ask | "I need a yes or no on the brand budget by Friday." |
| The disagreement | "I don't think we should run the Lisbon promo at all." |
| The correction | "The figure I sent on Tuesday was wrong." |
| The blunt fact | "Our onboarding is broken." |
| The question | "How much of the Q3 budget is actually committed?" |
| The thing that broke | "Call tracking has been dead on the Spanish pages since the 4th." |

Defaulting to the number every time replaces one formula with another. See "The fingerprints this layer creates" at the bottom.

Before:
> It's been a strange few weeks. Between the conference in Lisbon and the end-of-quarter push, I've barely had time to think. But one thing keeps coming back to me. Our onboarding is broken.

After:
> Our onboarding is broken. Forty percent of the accounts that signed up in July never completed the second step.

Before (client email):
> Hope this finds you well. As we head into the final stretch of the quarter, I wanted to share some thoughts on where we stand with the campaign.

After:
> Two things on the campaign before the quarter closes. The first one needs a decision from you by Friday.

## Fix 6: name something real

The move: for each claim, add the specific. If the specific is unavailable, weaken the claim to what you can support, or cut it.

| Vague | Named |
|---|---|
| industry benchmarks suggest a lower cost per lead is achievable | Redmann's Louisiana competitors are bidding $38 to $52 on the same terms; we're at $71 |
| a lot of teams run into this | Guava hit this at ETH Bogotá and solved it by moving check-in to the door |
| there's a well-known principle here | Ogilvy's rule: the headline is 80% of the spend |
| we saw strong performance | 412 registrations against a 300 target, 61% from the LATAM list |

This is the single most convertible fix, because in professional writing the named version is also the more useful version.

## Fix 7: assume shared history

The move: delete every clause that re-explains something the recipient lived through. Reference it by name instead.

Before:
> As you know, we've been managing your Google Ads account since June, focusing primarily on personal injury keywords in the Louisiana market, with the goal of reducing cost per acquisition while maintaining lead volume. With that context in mind, here are this month's results.

After:
> August numbers. CPA is down to $58 from $71, volume held at 43 leads.

If the email will be forwarded to someone without the history, put the context at the bottom under a heading, not at the top.

## Fix 8: cut the epilogue, break the flatness

Two moves, both aimed at the Claude fingerprint.

**Cut the epilogue.** Delete the final paragraph. Read the piece. If nothing is missing, it stays deleted.

Before:
> ...so the recommendation is to move the budget to search from Monday.
>
> Happy to walk through any of this on a call if it's useful. Either way, thanks for a strong quarter, and let me know how you'd like to proceed.

After:
> ...so the recommendation is to move the budget to search from Monday. Say yes or no by Friday and I'll set it up.

**Break the flatness.** In a flat draft every sentence carries the same weight. Pick the one thing that matters most and let it stand alone, short, in its own line. Let the surrounding sentences be longer than it.

Before:
> There were several issues with the launch, including the tracking gap, the delayed creative approval, and the CRM sync failure, all of which contributed to a slower start than planned.

After:
> Tracking was broken for nine days. Creative approval came late and the CRM sync failed twice, which we can absorb, but nine days of blind spend we cannot.

## Additions: address, jumps, ambivalence

Budget first. Under 40 words, none of these. Under 200, one. Under 800, two. Never all three in a piece shorter than a page.

**Direct address.** A parenthetical to the reader, or a line that acknowledges they are there.
> (You were on the call, so skip the next paragraph.)
> If you've already seen the deck, the only new thing is on slide 6.

**Time jump.** Reach back or forward without a smoothing transition. No "as previously mentioned," no "building on this."
> Anyway. We tried the opposite in June and it failed for a completely different reason.

**Ambivalence.** State the case against your own recommendation, or concede the self-interested part. It goes after the recommendation, in the same paragraph, never before it.
> The honest version: I want to run it because I want the case study. It also happens to be the cheapest option you have.

Two constraints on this one. In client-facing work, never pair it with an open item from Fix 2: one of the two reads as candour, both together read as a writer who has lost the thread. And skip it entirely where the deliverable is the decision itself, such as a proposal, a scope document, or a legal position.

**Emotion labels are not on this list.** Naming a feeling is a restore for personal essays, first-person posts, internal messages, and founder updates. It is not one for client work, where the right emotional register is usually none. The physicalised version still comes out; it just gets replaced by the fact, not by a feeling.

## The three tests every addition has to pass

Run these before an addition ships. Any failure means cut, not soften.

**The source test.** Point at where it came from: a line in the draft, a message in the thread, a figure in the data, a doubt the writer actually voiced. If you cannot point at it, you wrote fiction into someone's business correspondence. This is the operational version of "never fake the mess," and it is the only version that survives a deadline.

**The budget test.** Count the additions. Compare to the length band. Over budget means the least-sourced one comes out.

**The repetition test.** Look at the writer's last three pieces. If the same addition appears in all three, it has stopped being a human signal and become a house style. Skip it this time.

## Register playbooks

### Client email

Apply: 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8. Skip 2 unless the loose end is real, relevant, and carries owner, date, and consequence.

One addition, and it is a choice between two, never both: the open item or the concession after the recommendation. Both in the same email reads as a lost account.

The shape that works: decision or number first, evidence second, ask third, stop. Vary which of the seven openers carries the first line across a run of emails, or the shape itself becomes the tell.

### Internal chat, Slack, WhatsApp

Under 40 words, only three checks apply: no scaffolding, one concrete specific, no stated takeaway. Zero additions. The rest of the layer is not evaluable at this length, and forcing it produces invention.

Above 40 words, apply all eight, hard. Chat is where AI structure is most obvious, because a real chat message has no architecture at all. Cut the greeting, cut the sign-off, cut anything that reads as a paragraph. Fragments are correct. One message can be four words.

Before:
> Hi team! Quick update on the landing page. I've implemented the changes we discussed and everything is now live. Let me know if you spot any issues!

After:
> landing page is live
> the pricing table looks wrong on mobile, looking at it now

### LinkedIn or public post

Apply: 1, 4, 5, 6, 8. This is the format where the stated takeaway is most entrenched, because the whole genre is built on it. Cutting the final "the lesson is" line is the entire edit most of the time. Add: one direct address, one named specific.

### Client report or long-form update

Apply: 1, 5, 6, 7, 8. Skip 2 and 3 where the client is paying for a decision. A report that ends "I don't know" is a bad report. A report that ends "here's the recommendation, and here's what would change my mind" is honest and closed at once.

### Landing page or marketing copy

Apply: 1, 5, 6. Skip 2, 3, 7. Marketing copy is meant to be single-track and resolved; that is not an AI tell there, that is the job. The tells that matter are the vague claim and the mood-setting opener. Run `copy-that-sells` for generation, then this skill.

## Sequence-level editing

When editing more than one piece written to the same purpose (a five-email nurture flow, a week of participant comms, four ad variants):

1. Lay them side by side and diagnose the set.
2. Count how many open the same way. If more than half, rewrite the openers of the rest.
3. Check paragraph counts. If they are within one of each other across the set, force spread: make one twice as long, one half as long.
4. Check closing moves. If every one ends in a call to action of the same shape, change at least two.
5. Check register. If all five sit at the same politeness level, drop one to blunt.

The corpus evidence for this: five different models writing to the same prompt land in a tight cluster while human writers scatter. A sequence written by one model to one brief is that cluster in miniature.

## What to do when the fix costs clarity

Layer 2 fixes can make a piece worse. The failure modes, and what to do:

**The cut takeaway leaves the point unmade.** The evidence was too weak to carry it. Strengthen the evidence rather than restoring the sentence. If the evidence cannot be strengthened, restore the sentence and note it.

**The hanging thread reads as disorganised.** It was not a real loose end, it was noise. Cut it.

**The ambivalence reads as a lack of confidence.** Wrong placement. Ambivalence belongs after the recommendation, never before it. "Do X. The case against is Y." Not "I'm not sure, but maybe X."

**The named specific is wrong.** Worse than vague. Verify every number and name added during a Layer 2 edit, or mark it clearly for the writer to confirm in the Notes block.

**The blunt version reads as rude in the client's culture.** Register beats the rule. Note it and keep the politeness.

**The concession has become a formula.** If every recommendation this month arrived with "the case against is," it is no longer candour, it is a tic. Skip it and let the recommendation stand alone.

**The fragments are not the writer's.** Chat style imposed on someone who writes in sentences replaces their voice with the editor's. Restore the sentences.

In all seven cases, the rule loses. Say so in Notes. This file is a set of defaults, not a specification.

## The fingerprints this layer creates

Applying this file uniformly produces a recognisable register of its own. Five models writing to one brief cluster tightly; so does one editor running one checklist across fifty drafts. Watch for these in your own output:

- Every piece opens with a metric.
- Every piece closes with a date-stamped ask.
- The concession paragraph appears in every recommendation, one sentence, same slot.
- Chat fragments imposed on writers who do not use them.
- The open item that is always present and always exactly one paragraph.
- Uniform bluntness applied where the relationship did not earn it.

The defence is the same one the corpus points at: variance. Apply what the diagnosis actually found rather than the whole list, rotate the opener across a set, and let some pieces keep a rule unapplied. A ruleset applied without variation becomes the thing it was built to remove.
